PHD‑Hand

Printable Humanoid Dexterous Hand

A dexterous humanoid hand with three actuated degrees of freedom in every digit, driven entirely by linkages. A single 3D-printed flexure hinge at the root of each digit keeps the part count low enough to build on a budget 3D printer, at close to human size.

The flexure hinge

The one part that makes a fully linkage-driven 3-DOF digit simple.

The root of each digit is a single printed TPU part shaped like the strain relief on a cable connector. It tilts in two directions, for flexion and side-to-side motion, while resisting twist.

Because it bends gently instead of folding sharply, a 0.6 mm spring steel rod can pass straight through its center to drive the upper two joints in both directions. Bending the hinge doesn't push or pull that rod, so the root joint and the upper joints are completely decoupled. Tilting the root never disturbs the finger's curl, and the reverse holds too.

Two RC servos tilt the root through two rigid drive rods and four ball joints, a layout that grew out of the classic planar 5-bar. A cross 4-bar couples the upper two joints at a ratio of 0.77, within the 0.75 to 0.8 range measured in human fingers. Its coupling rod runs outside the middle phalanx, so it can be shaped to add some passive compliance during contact.

The root joint's forward kinematics is close to linear: flexion follows the average of the two servo angles and side tilt follows their difference. An affine fit stays within 1.8° over most of the range and within 2.7° everywhere.

What it can do

Thumb opposition, the full GRASP taxonomy, and in-hand manipulation.

In-hand manipulation

Relocate and flip. The hand shifts a jar in its grip until the index finger can reach the lid, then flips it open. That takes the thumb and index finger coordinating with the other three digits, plus the index finger's full range and strength.

Precision screwdriver. The thumb and middle finger alternate between retracting and spinning the driver without pushing it out of the index finger's hold.

Wrench worm screw. The thumb rolls the worm screw outward to close the jaws. Tendon-driven hands that rely on springs to extend their digits can't do this, so it shows true bidirectional actuation.

Control from the browser

A web GUI talks to the Mini Maestro directly. Nothing to install.

  • A 2-DOF pad per digit for jogging the root joint, plus a slider for the upper joints
  • Calibration for the zero, direction, and range of every DOF
  • Preset postures, and recording, saving, and replaying motions

The GUI and the motion trajectories, including the cube rotation above, were generated with Claude from a plain-text description of the hand and refined against the real prototype.

Build it

The full guide will come with the GitHub release. Here's the path from spool to working hand.

  1. Print the parts

    Print the flexure hinges and upper-joint strips in TPU 95A with the settings above, and the phalanges in PETG, or in TPU to make each digit body one piece.

  2. Cut the drive rods

    Cut the elastic drive rods from 0.6 mm 304 stainless spring steel wire, and shape the connectors with proper tooling, since a badly formed bend is the most likely place for a rod to break.

  3. Assemble the digits

    Each digit is a self-contained module with its three servos, so you can build, test, and swap them one at a time.

  4. Wire and calibrate

    Connect all 15 servos to the Mini Maestro using the channel map in the repo, then set each DOF's zero, direction, and range in the GUI.

  5. Run it

    Open the GUI in Chrome, connect to the Maestro, and try the preset postures or play back a recorded motion.
